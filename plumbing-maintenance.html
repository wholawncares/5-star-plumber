What is included in our plumbing maintenance visit

Every plumbing tune-up in Arlington Heights covers the same core checklist, whether you have a ranch in Hasbrook or a two-flat near the Business District. Add-ons like sewer camera inspection, hydro jetting, and backflow testing are included on our annual plan or quoted separately for one-time visits.

  • Water heater service

    We flush sediment from the tank, test the T&P valve, check the expansion tank charge, and inspect the anode rod. On a Rheem, AO Smith, or Bradford White tank, that flush is the single biggest thing you can do to extend its life.

  • Fixture and valve check

    Every toilet, faucet, and shut-off valve gets tested. We replace worn flappers, fill valves, and supply lines, and descale faucet aerators. A running toilet or a stiff valve gets fixed on the same visit.

  • Drain flow testing

    We run water through every drain and watch how fast it clears. Slow lines get a drain snake or auger. If the main line is slow, we recommend a sewer camera inspection to see what is causing it.

  • Pressure and leak check

    A pressure gauge on an exterior hose bib tells us your static and dynamic pressure. We read the water meter before and after the visit to confirm there is no hidden leak pulling water when nothing is running.

  • Sump pump cycle test

    We fill the pit and watch the pump cycle, check the float, and inspect the discharge line and check valve. A Zoeller or similar pump that hesitates gets flagged before spring rains arrive.

  • Written report

    You get photos, pressure readings, component ages, and a list of anything that needs attention in the next 6 to 12 months. It is your building's plumbing history in one document.

Tools and methods we use on every visit

Move across a circle, or focus it and press Enter, for an illustrative thermal view of the photograph.

  • Sewer camera (RIDGID)

    Pushed through the main lateral to record the interior of the pipe. We show you the footage so you can see roots, cracks, or scale for yourself.

  • Hydro jetter

    High-pressure water scours grease, scale, and roots from drain lines without chemicals. Used when a camera shows buildup that snaking will not clear.

  • Leak detection equipment

    Acoustic and pressure-based tools that isolate hidden leaks in walls, slabs, and underground lines without opening the whole floor.

  • Pressure gauge

    Threaded onto a hose bib to read static and dynamic water pressure. High pressure above 80 psi is a leading cause of pinhole leaks and fixture failure.

  • Drain snake and auger

    Mechanical clearing of kitchen, bath, and laundry clogs. We use the right head for the pipe size and material so we do not damage the line.

Thermal views are illustrative, generated from the photographs.

Methods

  • Pipe wrench and tube cutter

    Standard hand tools for opening supply lines, replacing shut-off valves, and cutting copper or PEX tubing cleanly for repair.

  • PEX crimping tool

    Used to make code-correct PEX connections when replacing a failed section of tubing or adding a new fixture line.

  • Meter-based leak verification

    We record the water meter reading with every fixture off, wait, and read again. A moving meter with nothing running confirms a hidden leak and tells us how fast it is losing water.

  • Water heater flush and anode inspection

    We drain the tank through a hose to remove sediment, then pull the anode rod and measure how much material is left. Replacing a consumed anode rod is the cheapest way to add years to a tank.

  • Camera-first drain diagnosis

    Before we jet or snake a main line, we run a camera. Seeing the pipe interior tells us whether the problem is grease, roots, scale, or a break, and that determines the right fix.

  • Pressure-reducing valve adjustment

    When static pressure reads above 80 psi, we test the pressure-reducing valve and adjust or replace it. Bringing pressure into the 50 to 70 psi range protects every fixture and supply line in the house.

How plumbing maintenance runs in Arlington Heights

Arlington Heights plumbing has two personalities. The older neighborhoods, like Old Arlington Heights and Scarsdale, were built mostly between 1950 and 1975 with copper supply lines and clay or cast iron sewer laterals. Those systems are durable but they scale, pit, and root.

The newer subdivisions around Arlington Market and Creekside use PEX tubing and PVC drains, which fail differently: crimp rings loosen, manifolds develop pinholes, and pressure-reducing valves drift high. Our maintenance visits are built around which era your house is from. Winter matters here too.

We get single-digit nights and hard freeze-thaw cycles, which is why fall maintenance focuses on hose bib shut-offs, exterior wall insulation, and sump pump readiness. Cook County requires backflow testing on irrigation and commercial systems, and we handle that paperwork.

  • Old Arlington Heights: Mostly pre-1970 homes with copper supply lines and clay sewer laterals. Pinhole leaks and root intrusion are the two most common maintenance findings here.
  • Scarsdale: Mature trees and older laterals make root intrusion common. We recommend a sewer camera inspection on the first maintenance visit for any home here.
  • Arlington Market: Newer construction with PEX tubing and PVC drains. We watch for loose crimp rings, manifold issues, and pressure-reducing valves that drift above 80 psi.
  • Hasbrook: Mix of ranch homes and two-flats. Sump pumps and laundry room supply lines are the two components we see fail most often in this area.
  • Miner Street Corridor: A dense mix of commercial and residential buildings. Backflow testing and after-hours scheduling are common requests from property managers here.
  • Hard water scaling in water heaters and fixtures: Arlington Heights water leaves mineral scale in tanks and on aerators. Without an annual flush, a water heater loses efficiency and can fail years early.

Problems a preventive plumbing maintenance visit solves

Most plumbing failures in Arlington Heights give you weeks of warning. A preventive plumbing maintenance Arlington Heights IL visit is how you hear that warning in time to act on it.

  • Hidden slab leakHidden slab leak under the floorA slab leak can run for months, warping flooring, growing mold, and adding hundreds to your water bill before you notice a warm spot or a spike in usage. We read the meter with everything off and use leak detection equipment to isolate the loss. If it is under the slab, we locate it precisely, open the smallest possible area, repair the line, and pressure-test before closing.
  • Water heater failureWater heater about to failA tank with a spent anode rod rusts from the inside. By the time you see water on the floor, the tank is done and you are buying a new one on an emergency schedule. We pull the anode rod on every maintenance visit and check its condition. If it is more than 75 percent consumed, we replace it for a fraction of a new heater. We also flush sediment that shortens tank life.
  • Root intrusionRoot intrusion in the sewer lateralRoots enter through joints in clay pipe and grow into a mat that catches paper and grease. The first sign is usually a backed-up laundry tub or floor drain, often during a holiday. A sewer camera inspection shows us exactly where roots are entering. We hydro jet the line to cut them out, then set you up on annual maintenance so we can monitor the joint and catch regrowth early.
  • Frozen pipesFrozen pipes in exterior wallsArlington Heights winters drop into single digits. Supply lines in exterior walls or unheated crawl spaces freeze, and the burst usually shows up when they thaw. During fall maintenance we check pipe insulation, heat tape, and hose bib shut-offs. We flag vulnerable runs and can add insulation or reroute lines before the first hard freeze.
  • Sump pump failureFailing sump pump before springA sump pump that has not run all winter can seize or have a stuck float. The first heavy spring rain then floods the basement, often destroying stored items and finished space. We cycle-test the pump, check the float and check valve, and inspect the discharge line for freezing or blockage. If the pump is near end of life, we replace it on your schedule, not during a storm.
